<Phase type="activation">

Picture a hairdresser you have gone to for years. They move to another city. You go to a new salon.

**What will you bring to the new hairdresser — a photo of the haircut, or nothing?** Think about what you were really going to the old one for.

</Phase>

<Phase type="reflection">

Go back to the new salon. Someone shows a photo, and the haircut comes out the same: they had a way of doing it. Someone says “it's not the same”: they had a relationship with the hairdresser.

**Both things are real. But only one can be handed to another person or another format.**

</Phase>

<Phase type="concept">

## Two columns

A client gets two different things from a session.

**From the method** — what repeats and what they can name themselves. An exercise, an order of steps, a question after which things get clearer. A result that shows even without you.

**From the relationship** — attention, trust, the feeling of being listened to. This is not a side effect. It is the strength of the “one to one” step.

## When a bond becomes a ceiling

A bond is not a problem. The trouble starts when the client cannot name anything besides it. They come “to you”, not for something.

Then the practice rests on you entirely. It cannot be repeated for two people, handed to an apprentice, or paused for a holiday without loss.

## What the codes say

The Global Code of Ethics for coaches and mentors (December 2025 edition) asks practitioners to actively support the client's independence and self-reliance. The length of the work should match its goals.

The ICF guide on referring a client (2018) names a signal in the practitioner: the feeling that the client depends on them too much. With such a signal you go to a supervisor.

## Through the client's eyes

A client may say: “I don't come for the exercises, I come to you.” This is honest and warm. But then without you they may be left with nothing.

**A client being attached does not prove you are a good practitioner.** A good practitioner leaves the client something that stays with them even without the practitioner.

<Phase type="practice">

Go back to your “A map of your practice”.

1. Choose three clients: a long-standing one, a new one, and the one you think about most.
2. For each, fill in two columns: what they get from the method and what from the relationship.
3. If the “from the method” column is empty, do not invent anything. An empty cell is material for module 02.
4. Answer for yourself: what will each of the three be left with if you go away for a month?

</Phase>
