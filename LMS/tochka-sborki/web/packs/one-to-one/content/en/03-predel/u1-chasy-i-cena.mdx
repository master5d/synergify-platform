<Phase type="activation">

Picture a taxi driver. The meter shows the price of the ride. Between rides, he waits for an order and drives empty to the other side of town.

**What is an hour of his work really worth?** Think about what the meter sees and what it does not.

</Phase>

<Phase type="reflection">

Go back to the car. The meter counts only the ride with a passenger. The empty miles and the waiting are part of the day, but not of the receipt.

**The price of a ride is only half the truth about the hour.** A session is the same: the client pays for an hour of conversation, but you work longer.

</Phase>

<Phase type="concept">

## Two kinds of hours

**Client hours** — the sessions people pay for.

**Hours outside sessions** — everything without which there would be no sessions: booking and messages, bookkeeping, preparation, promotion, taxes.

The second kind does not show in the price of a session, but it is part of your week.

## What the data shows

There is no open data on hours outside sessions for coaches and bodywork practitioners. There is data from a neighbouring profession.

A 2026 study surveyed 862 independent practitioners in mental health. They named promotion (48 percent), taxes (42) and documentation (38) as their most stressful duties. Among those burned out, 37 percent spend six or more hours a week on work outside sessions.

## A worked example

The practitioner runs 20 sessions a week at 60 units each. The revenue of the week is 1200.

Another 8 hours go to work outside sessions. In all, there are 28 working hours.

The price of a working hour: 1200 divided by 28, about 43. **Not 60, but 43.**

## Half the truth

The phrase “my hour costs 60” is true only of a session hour. For decisions about price and the limit, you need the price of every working hour.
