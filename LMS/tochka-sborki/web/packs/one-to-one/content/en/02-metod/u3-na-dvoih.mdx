<Phase type="activation">

Picture a conversation for two at a kitchen table. It is late and quiet, and things get said that would not be said by day. The door opens, and a third person sits down at the table.

**What changes in the conversation?** Think about what goes away, and what perhaps appears.

</Phase>

<Phase type="reflection">

Go back to the table. Someone has started to talk less. The time has been split three ways. Some words were left unsaid.

**But the third person brought a view the two did not have.** Two people are no longer “one to one”, but not yet a group either.

</Phase>

<Phase type="concept">

## What breaks with two

When there are two clients, three things change.

**Time.** Attention is split, and each person gets fewer minutes.
**Confidentiality.** One hears what the other says.
**Pace.** Two people rarely move at the same speed.

That is why each step of the method is tested on its own. There are three answers: **yes, with a change, no.**

## What research says

There is little research on group coaching and no systematic review. But a few conclusions repeat.

Reviews say: rules and confidentiality are agreed at the first meeting. Participants are selected in advance. The group format is not for everyone.

In a randomised study with 252 teachers, paired coaching fell behind individual coaching in places. After correcting for multiple comparisons, the difference disappeared.

## A worked example

The leader tested her six steps on two people.

- Starting with the breath — **yes**.
- The question “what have you come with” — **with a change**: five minutes each.
- A body scan — **yes**.
- An exercise for the request — **with a change**: one shared exercise for similar requests.
- “What did you notice” — **yes**, and even richer with two.
- Hands-on work — **no**: it stays for one-to-one sessions.

## Not the same, only cheaper

In a group you cannot do the same as one to one, just for more people. **A meeting for two is a different format with its own laws.** The method helps you see what carries over from it.

<Phase type="practice">

Go back to the “A method on one page” template.

1. For each step, fill in the “for two” column: yes, with a change, or no.
2. For each “with a change”, write what exactly changes.
3. Count how many steps got “yes” or “with a change”.
4. If more than half of the steps did, you already have a basis for a meeting for 2–7 people.

Do not throw away the steps with “no”. They are why some clients will stay one to one.

</Phase>
