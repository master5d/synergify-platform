<Phase type="activation">

Picture an archer shooting into an empty sky. The arrow flies beautifully and far. But not one comes back asking "where did I land?".

**Think about who your retreat description is aimed at.** One person, or everyone at once.

</Phase>

<Phase type="reflection">

Go back to the archer. They do not need a better bow. They need a target.

**A description "for everyone who is searching for themselves" is a shot into the sky.** A person reads it and does not recognise themselves, so they cannot decide whether to come.

</Phase>

<Phase type="concept">

## One person instead of "everyone"

A week-long retreat costing several thousand dollars is rarely bought from one post. A person decides slowly, and decides when they recognise themselves in the description. So it is worth starting with one concrete guest, not a broad audience.

One guest is not a limit on the group. Different people will come to the retreat. But a text addressed to one person is heard by others like them too.

## A change instead of a list of activities

A guest does not buy a schedule of practices. They buy who they will be when they come home: rested, clear, with a decision, with a new rhythm. The list "yoga, meditation, walks" describes the week, not the change.

Ask yourself: what does a person arrive with, and what do they leave with. That is the essence of the offer.

## The value is already in the leading

Many leaders try to add value: one more excursion, one more practice, one more gift. Each addition is a new cost line.

**The main value is the space you hold.** Additions make sense if they serve the guest's change, not if they are there "to have more".

## When is part of the offer too

Dates decide for the guest before the text does. A week over a long holiday weekend, the school break or New Year rules out people with children, jobs or family plans. The season decides whether you can walk, swim or sit by the fire.

Before you name the dates, check three things: holidays and school breaks in your guests' country, the weather and season at the place, and how long the trip there takes. Awkward dates are a common reason a good offer does not sell.

## What is not here

The course does not teach pressing on pain and does not teach urgency tactics. The guest description is there to say honestly: this week is for these people, and not for those. Turning away someone it does not suit saves time for both.

</Phase>

<Phase type="practice">

Describe one guest in five lines. Not a "target audience", but one person.

1. Who they are: age, occupation, where they live.
2. What they have now: where they are in life.
3. What change they seek: what they want to leave with.
4. What holds them back: money, time, fear, distrust.
5. Why they would come with you, and not to a similar retreat.

Reread the first paragraph of your retreat description. Would this person recognise themselves in it?

</Phase>
