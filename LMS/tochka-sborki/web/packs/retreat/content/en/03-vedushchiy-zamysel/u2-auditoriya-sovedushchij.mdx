<Phase type="activation">

Picture inviting friends to dinner together with a neighbour friend. The neighbour has a big circle of friends, you have a small one. You agreed that his guests would fill the table.

**In the evening, the table is mostly your people.** Think about why it might have turned out that way.

</Phase>

<Phase type="reflection">

Go back to the table. His guests knew him, but did not know why they needed this dinner. Your guests came to you.

**People go on a retreat to a person they trust.** A partner's large audience does not mean it will travel to the two of you.

</Phase>

<Phase type="concept">

## Who will really fill the group

Leaders often invite a co-leader for their audience: more followers, their own studio or a book. Sometimes it works. But leaders also tell the opposite stories: the group was gathered almost entirely by the people of the one whose audience was smaller.

The reason is simple. The decision to go away for a week is made out of trust in a particular person. The partner's followers know the partner, not your shared week.

So estimate the group by your own people: who has been to your classes, who has written to you, who has asked about trips. The partner's audience is a bonus, not a plan.

## A co-leader means a contract

The most common mistake is not agreeing in writing with someone close. "We are friends" works until the first difficult situation. Leaders tell of co-leaders who turned into holidaymakers at the retreat, and of splitting money that was never discussed in advance.

What is worth writing down before sales:

- who is responsible for what before the retreat, on site and after;
- how money is split: revenue, costs, and the loss if there is one;
- who leads which practices and who holds the group in a hard moment;
- expectations of behaviour: alcohol, presence, time on site;
- what happens if one of you cannot go.

Expectations are worth naming concretely, down to "on site 20 minutes before the session". Nobody rises to expectations that were never voiced.

</Phase>

<Phase type="practice">

1. Write down how many people from your circle can really come: those who attended classes, wrote, asked.
2. Separately write down how many you think the partner would bring, and honestly divide that number by two.
3. Compare the sum with your retreat's break-even from the calculator.
4. If you work with a co-leader, make a list of expectations covering the five points above.
5. Send the list to the co-leader before you start selling.

</Phase>
