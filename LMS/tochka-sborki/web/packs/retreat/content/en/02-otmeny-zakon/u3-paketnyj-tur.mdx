Picture yourself baking bread for the neighbours. While it is three loaves a week, you are a neighbour with an oven. When it is a hundred loaves with a price tag, people see you as a bakery.

**You bake the same bread. What changes is what you are taken for.** Think about the moment when a retreat leader stops being "just a leader".

</Phase>

<Phase type="reflection">

Go back to the bread. The bakery does not need to change the recipe. It needs to follow rules that a neighbour does not.

**A retreat with accommodation and a program may turn out to be a package holiday.** Then the rules for tour organisers apply to you, even though you keep leading practices.

</Phase>

<Phase type="concept">

## What a package is

As of 2026-10, the EU has the Package Travel Directive 2015/2302. The United Kingdom applies similar regulations from 2018. A package is at least two different travel services for one trip, put together by one seller or sold at a single price.

Accommodation plus "another tourist service" becomes a package in two cases. The second is a significant part of the cost, or it is advertised as the essence of the offer. The UK guidance gives a benchmark: about 25 % of the cost.

For a retreat where the program is the essence, this is a **likely package** even without transfers. That is a conclusion from the text of the rules, not a court ruling. Check it with a lawyer in your country.

## Who the organiser is

The organiser is whoever puts the package together and sells it to the guest. If the guest pays the leader for "everything included", the organiser is most likely the leader, not the venue.

The exceptions are narrow: trips shorter than a day without an overnight stay, and occasional not-for-profit trips for a limited group.

## What the organiser must do

If the retreat is a package, the organiser takes on duties:

- **protecting guests' money against your own insolvency:** insurance, a guarantee or a trust account, so that guests get their money back;
- **a full refund without a charge** if there are extraordinary circumstances at the destination;
- **the guest's right to cancel at any time** for a reasonable and justified fee;
- **the guest's right to pass the place** to another person;
- **cancelling because of a short group** — only with a full refund; for trips over six days, notice is 20 days ahead;
- **raising the price** only for agreed reasons, including the exchange rate; above 8 % the guest may withdraw.

## Why this matters to both sides

There is a known case: a retreat centre closed after a natural disaster. Its board admitted owing leaders' groups about 200,000 dollars in deposits ([source](https://www.hawaiitribune-herald.com/?p=223919)). The money was not kept in a separate account.

The lesson for the leader: large sums held by a venue without protection are your risk. For the venue: do not fund running costs with other people's deposits. This is not legal advice.

<Phase type="practice">

Go through the checklist for your retreat. Answer each question yes or no.

1. Does the retreat include an overnight stay?
2. Do you sell accommodation and the program together, at one price or through one link?
3. Is the program the main thing in the offer, the first thing described?
4. Are there residents of the EU or the UK among the guests?
5. Do guests pay you, not the venue?
6. Do you run such retreats regularly and for money?

If the answer to questions 1–4 is "yes", the retreat is probably a package holiday. If 5–6 are also "yes", the organiser is probably you. Write down which of the duties you already cover, and a question for a lawyer about protecting guests' money.

</Phase>
