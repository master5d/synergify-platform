Picture a sign on a shop door: "No refunds". You bought something, and it fell apart on the doorstep.

**The sign is there, but nobody believes it cancels common sense.** Think about what it actually says, and what it only seems to say.

<Phase type="reflection">

Go back to the sign. The word on the door does not settle the dispute. What settles it is what happened and how much each side lost.

**A "non-refundable" label in retreat terms works the same way.** It names an intention, but it does not guarantee that the money stays with you in every circumstance.

</Phase>

<Phase type="concept">

## What the law says — as of 2026-10

The rules differ by country, but the general sense is similar. This is not legal advice: check your country's rules with a lawyer.

**United Kingdom.** The Consumer Rights Act 2015 treats one kind of term as potentially unfair. It lets the seller keep a cancelling customer's money. Yet the customer gets no equal sum if the seller is the one who cancels. The details are in guidance CMA37 of 22 July 2026 from the UK Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) ([document](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6a609329b00f3323bf1a23f3/unfair_contract_terms_guidance.pdf), para. 6.62). A genuine deposit may be kept in full if it secures the booking and its terms are clear and narrow. Such a deposit is "not normally more than a small percentage of the price". A large prepayment kept in all circumstances may turn out to be a disguised penalty. The difference is simple: keeping what the leader really lost looks like compensation, keeping more than that looks like a penalty.

**European Union.** The Unfair Terms Directive 93/13 lists similar terms as possibly unfair. They are keeping what was paid and a disproportionately high compensation from a customer who withdraws.

**United States.** There is no single rule. Courts usually treat a retained sum as agreed damages. If it is clearly larger than the likely loss, it may be held a penalty and not enforced. The label "non-refundable" alone does not decide this.

## There is usually no 14-day right to withdraw from a retreat

In the EU and the UK, accommodation and leisure services on specific dates are excluded from the right to withdraw from a distance purchase. So the guest's cancellation terms are set by your contract with them. And that contract is checked for fairness.

## "First payment" and "deposit"

Some leaders deliberately call a guest's first payment not a "deposit" but a "first payment". The regulator's logic explains the difference. A deposit is a small sum for the booking that can fairly be kept. A first payment is part of the price, and on cancellation it follows the scale and the real losses.

## What a fair term looks like

The regulator gives an example of a term that is more likely to be fair. It is a table. Cancelling is free up to a certain day. After that, a percentage of the price is a proportionate contribution to the organiser's costs.

Build yours the same way:

- a small first payment secures the place, and the terms say plainly when it becomes non-refundable;
- then a scale by days, tied to your obligations to the venue;
- if the place is resold to another guest, the amount kept goes down;
- a transfer to the next retreat as a kind alternative to a refund.

<Phase type="practice">

1. Write out your current guest cancellation terms, if you have any.
2. Find the words "non-refundable", "no refunds", "in any case" in them.
3. For each such place, write down which real loss it covers: a venue payment, purchases, your hours.
4. Rewrite the terms as a table by days before arrival. Tie the steps to the venue's dates from the previous lesson.
5. Add a line on reselling the place and a line on transferring to the next retreat.

</Phase>
