<Phase type="activation">

Picture letting your house for a week to a group of strangers. They sent a beautiful letter and photos. You know nothing else about them.

**For a week their guests will live in the house, and you will answer to them too.** Think about what you would want to know before handing over the keys.

</Phase>

<Phase type="reflection">

Go back to the letter with the photos. A beautiful presentation says how a person sells, not how they lead.

**A venue does not choose a client, it chooses a partner for a week.** Its reviews, its staff and its future groups depend on the leader.

</Phase>

<Phase type="concept">

## What can be checked before the contract

**The leader's own audience.** Who has been to their classes and trips, how many people actually write to them. A group is gathered by the leader's own circle. The audience of a co-leader or partner is a bonus that a minimum cannot be built on.

**The program and practice cards.** A practice catalogue is the first thing a venue sees from a leader. Promises like "will heal" and "opens abilities" are a signal. So is the absence of touch and load. An honest card shows which hall is needed, how much time, and which practices might affect guests.

**The contract with a co-leader.** If there are two leaders, ask how they split roles and money. A co-leader without a contract is a common cause of breakdowns on site.

**Past groups.** How many retreats the leader has run, how many guests came, whether there were cancellations. Guests' reviews matter more than colleagues' reviews.

## A model to fit the leader

A new leader with a small audience suits a model with a small minimum, or a buyout of a small venue. An experienced leader with a steady group can be offered a full campus. The model is fitted to the leader, not the other way round.

</Phase>

<Phase type="practice">

Draw up a checklist for assessing a leader and apply it to a teaching example. The leader has run two day trips and has 300 followers. The program has 6 practices with no load stated, and the co-leader's audience is ten times larger.

1. Write five questions for the leader: audience, program, co-leader, past groups, sales plan.
2. Mark what in the teaching example already raises a question.
3. Ask the leader for practice cards using the course template.
4. Choose a model and a minimum that suit this leader.
5. Write down which answer would make you say no.

</Phase>
