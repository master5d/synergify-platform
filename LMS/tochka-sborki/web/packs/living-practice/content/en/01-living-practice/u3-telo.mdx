<Phase type="activation">

Recall the last time you learned about your own state from the body rather than from the head. A jaw gone stiff by evening. Shoulders held up near the ears for a whole conversation. A stomach that clenched before you'd understood what the letter was about.

Don't work out now what it meant. **Register only the moment of recognition: the body knew first.**

Hold that. What follows is about the fact that this knowledge has a price, and that you can't go after it heedlessly.

</Phase>

<Phase type="reflection">

Change the lens. Not a "scanner" methodically covering an object edge to edge, but **a torch in an unfamiliar house.**

A torch is used selectively. The beam can be turned aside. It can be switched off while you stand in the general light. You can walk out onto the porch and look at the house from outside. Nobody is obliged to sweep the beam through every room in sequence when cold air comes out of one of them.

**Notice the difference in the expectation itself.** A scanner must cover everything. A torch need not. The whole construction of this step rests on that difference.

</Phase>

<Phase type="concept">

## Why attention to the body at all

The body gives what the breath cannot: scale. Breath is a narrow point; the body is a map. On that map you can see what thought reports inaccurately: where tension is held, where it's cold, where nothing registers at all.

And that is exactly why this is not a practice that is safe by default.

## The honest caveat that usually gets put at the end

**With a history of trauma, prolonged uninterrupted concentration on bodily sensation is not a suitable format.** Not "harder", not "needs preparation" — not suitable. Sustained focused attention on the body in trauma survivors can provoke flashbacks, dissociation, and re-experiencing.

This is not a hypothesis of the course. **David Treleaven, *Trauma-Sensitive Mindfulness* (W. W. Norton, 2018)** is the practical frame the field of trauma-sensitive practice stands on: five domains (attention, arousal, relationship, dissociation, social context) and 36 concrete modifications to exercises. It is a practitioner's book rather than a randomised trial; the honest way to cite it is as a working frame, not as proof.

The second source is population data. In the sample of **Goldberg, Lam, Britton and Davidson (*Psychotherapy Research*, 2022; 434 people with meditation experience)**, **re-experiencing traumatic material** is among the most frequent adverse effects, and **adverse childhood events** appear as a risk factor.

## The window of tolerance

Treleaven's central notion is the **window of tolerance**. Inside it, a person feels and stays themselves: able to be present with a sensation without falling into it. Outside the window there is agitation and panic on one side, numbness and "I'm not here" on the other.

Attention to the body is useful inside the window and harmful past its edge. The task is not to "finish the scan" but to **stay inside the window**, even if that means cutting the scan off halfway.

## Four modifications that make the format safer

All four come from Treleaven, and none of them is an option "for the sensitive" — they are the course standard:

1. **Eyes open.** Not "you may open them if anxious", but open by default, soft gaze on the floor. Closed eyes remove the support of external reality — the very thing that helps you not fall in.
2. **Alternating inward and outward.** After each region, one cycle on an external object: a chair, a plant, a window, an animal. This isn't "distraction", it's a load-bearing part of the exercise.
3. **The right to stop at any moment.** With no explanation, no "let me finish", no going back to the place where it got bad.
4. **Short duration.** For a beginner, a shortened pass, not forty minutes millimetre by millimetre.

A fifth, separate one: **movement as a way back.** If you're being carried out of the window — stand, walk, shake out the arms. Walking and gentle movement practice are recommended by Treleaven precisely as a return to the window, not as a substitute for "real practice".

## What is not done here

No "working with the holding pattern", no "releasing trauma from the body", no "breathing into the pain". These are not meditation techniques — they are the language of therapeutic methods, and without a clinical context they are not used here. If more comes up from the body than fits into a short session, that is a reason to see a living professional, not a reason to practise longer.

</Phase>

<Phase type="practice">

**Session — 12 minutes.** A shortened pass: six regions, not thirty.

1. Sit. Eyes open, soft gaze on the floor a metre ahead. Timer — 12 minutes.
2. For two breaths, simply register that you're sitting. Find one external object with your eyes and keep it in mind — that's your point of return.
3. Then six regions, about a minute and a half each: **feet → hands → belly → chest → shoulders and neck → face and jaw.**
4. One question in each region: what is here right now? Warmth, cold, pressure, tingling, emptiness, "nothing registers" — all of these are valid answers. "Nothing registers" is an observation too.
5. **After each region, move your gaze to your external object and take one calm breath.** Then go on to the next region.
6. If a region becomes uncomfortable, leave it immediately. Not "stay with it a little longer". Come back to the object, breathe, then move on or end the session.
7. If you're carried further than that, stand up and walk. The session is over at that point, and that is its normal ending, not a failed one.
8. At the chime, sit a few seconds, look at the window or the room, and stand.

After the session, record in the journal: which region turned out to be the loudest, where "nothing registered", how many times you used the external point of return, and whether there was a place you had to leave.

**Talking about the session.** Press "Learn with AI" and hand it those four answers. A good question for the companion: "did I leave that region because it was uncomfortable, or because it was boring?" Telling those two apart is half the skill. The companion does not interpret sensations and does not explain what a tight jaw "means"; such readings are outside its remit, and step seven covers why.

</Phase>
