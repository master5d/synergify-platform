<Phase type="activation">

Picture a room nothing has been carried out of in a long time. Not a dump — an ordinary wardrobe, a top shelf, a desk drawer. Things live there that once had nowhere else to go, and since then they simply are.

**Now recall the moment you opened such a drawer by accident.** Looking for one thing, you came across another — a letter, a photograph, somebody else's object. The second before you decided what to do with it.

Don't analyse what was in there. Hold that second itself: quiet made audible something that had never been deliberately hidden. It just lay there silently while it was noisy.

</Phase>

<Phase type="reflection">

Change the lens. Not a drawer, but **a window opened in a cold room.**

While the window is shut, the air in the room seems fine. You open it, and the first thing that happens is that it gets worse: cold, a draught, an urge to shut it again. Nobody concludes from this that the window is broken, and nobody concludes that the room should be aired until you pass out.

Everyone knows two things about windows at once: **a draught is not a malfunction**, and **airing makes sense in measured amounts**. Both halves hold together.

**Notice that with practice, people usually hold only one of the halves.** Some say "it got worse, so the method is bad". Others say "it got worse, so it's working — endure". Both answers are equally imprecise, and this step is about where the line between them runs.

</Phase>

<Phase type="concept">

## The thing that makes practice do anything at all

Practice removes noise. Along with the noise, what the noise was covering becomes audible. That is not a side effect of successful practice — it is its mechanism. A course that promises only calm is selling a closed window.

But it does not follow that any worsening is useful. Now the numbers.

## What showed up once somebody finally asked

**Britton, Lindahl, Cooper, Canby and Palitsky (*Clinical Psychological Science*, 2021)** — 96 participants in an eight-week programme, interviewed by an independent assessor on a 44-item instrument:

- **83%** reported at least one practice-related effect;
- **58%** reported effects with negative valence;
- **37%** reported a negative impact on everyday functioning;
- **6–14%** reported persistent bad effects associated with signs of arousal dysregulation.

**Goldberg, Lam, Britton and Davidson (*Psychotherapy Research*, 2022)** — a US population sample, 434 people with meditation experience:

- **32.3%** reported adverse effects overall; **50.0%** reported at least one specific effect;
- lasting a month or longer — **10.4%**; functional impairment — **10.6%**; impairment lasting a month or more — **1.2%**;
- most frequently: anxiety, **re-experiencing traumatic material**, emotional hypersensitivity;
- a risk factor: **adverse childhood events**.

And one detail from there that is rarely retold: **people who reported harm were just as glad they had practised as everyone else.** Both halves of the truth are one truth.

The review by **Matko and Van Dam (*Current Opinion in Psychology*, 2026)** sums up the spread like this: **25–87%** of practitioners report adverse effects, and **3–37%** report functional impairment; where transient discomfort ends and lasting harm begins, the field has not yet drawn.

The spread is huge because different studies count different things. Two conclusions are stable: **unpleasant effects are common**, and **persistent harm is rare** — on the order of a few per cent up to the mid-teens.

## The window of tolerance — a second time, and not by accident

From step three: inside the window (Treleaven, 2018) a person feels and stays themselves. Past the edge there is either revving up (panic, trembling, being wired) or numbness and an absence of self.

The working rule: **difficult experience inside the window is material. Difficult experience past the edge of the window is a signal to stop.** There is no benefit in "toughing it out" past the edge, and no study has ever shown one.

## Stop signals

End the session and return to ordinary things if any of these appear:

- a sense that you or the world are unreal, which doesn't pass after practice;
- traumatic memories or flashbacks surfacing;
- mounting anxiety, panic, trembling, insomnia, a wired feeling;
- fear that you "won't get back";
- unusual perceptions, confusion;
- **any thoughts of harming yourself.**

The last item is not "a reason to lower the dose" but a reason to leave the learning scenario immediately and reach a living person: a professional or a crisis line. Neither this text nor the course's AI companion is crisis care.

## The words you explain difficulty to yourself with are an intervention

The most unexpected thing in the evidence for this step is this. The paper **"The roles and impacts of worldviews in the context of meditation-related challenges" (*Transcultural Psychiatry*, 2022)** showed that **the interpretive frame itself changes the trajectory and duration of difficulties.** "This is progress, endure it" and "this is pathology, something is wrong with me" are not two descriptions of one event, but two different outcomes.

Hence the course frame, said out loud:

> **A difficult experience doesn't mean you're practising badly. And it doesn't mean you've advanced. It means the dose is too large right now — and it needs to come down.**

That is exactly why "resistance" in an honest course is not read as a defect in the student. A format where refusing to practise is declared your problem reproduces the very frame that, in the paper cited above, lengthens difficulties.

## Two ways back

When a session has rocked you but the stop signals haven't appeared, there are two working returns:

1. **Movement.** Stand, walk, shake out the arms, look out of the window. In Treleaven, gentle movement and walking are recommended outright as a way back into the window of tolerance.
2. **Goodwill practice** (*mettā*, as the Pali tradition calls it). Short phrases of well-wishing for yourself and others. The evidence base is small but real: a randomised trial on self-criticism (*Clinical Psychology & Psychotherapy*, 2014) and a randomised trial on compassion fatigue in nurses (*Holistic Nursing Practice*, 2023). **An honest caveat: in some people this practice brings up grief or anger rather than warmth.** If so, that's not an error of execution; simply choose movement this time.

## Where to go if it persists beyond the session

If the heaviness reaches past the session and holds for several days, ease off practice and contact a living professional. That it "reaches past the session" is not in itself unusual: in Lindahl 2017, **88%** of difficulties extended beyond formal practice. That is a property of the phenomenon, not a sign that you're doing something wrong. But it is a reason to reduce, not to add.

The kinds of professionals worth going to: a psychotherapist, a psychiatrist, a trauma specialist. The course substitutes for none of them and issues no referrals. With thoughts of self-harm — a crisis line immediately; in the US that is 988, elsewhere the local line.

</Phase>

<Phase type="practice">

**Session — 12 minutes, of which the last three are the return.** Today the practice is built so that the exit is inside it rather than bolted on afterwards.

1. Sit. Eyes open, soft gaze on the floor. Find an external point of return with your eyes, as in step three. Timer — 12 minutes.
2. The first five minutes: breath, as in step two. Don't go looking for anything.
3. The next four minutes: stay with the breath, but **don't turn away if something surfaces.** Don't go towards it and don't go deeper. One label, one word (step four) — and back to the breath.
4. **A window check, once in the middle of that stretch:** am I here, in the room, and finding this unpleasant? Or am I already being carried off? The first — continue. The second — go to point 6 immediately.
5. If any stop signal appears, the session is over right now. That is correct execution of the exercise, not a breakdown.
6. **The last three minutes are the return.** Choose one: either stand and walk slowly, feeling your feet, or stay seated and repeat inwardly three short well-wishes — for yourself, for someone close, and for someone neutral. If the phrases land as heaviness rather than warmth, just go and walk.
7. Finish standing, having looked at the room or out of the window.

After the session, record in the journal: did anything surface, which label fitted it, where you were relative to the edge of the window, which return you used and how it worked.

**Talking about the session.** Press "Learn with AI" and hand it those four points. Keep in mind what the companion does and does not do: it helps you break the session into parts and find words, but it **does not interpret the content of what surfaced, does not work with traumatic material, and does not conduct a crisis conversation.** If today was heavy past the edge of the window, that conversation belongs with a person, not a model. Step seven covers where that boundary comes from and why it isn't our caution but a requirement of the field — and, in one US state, outright law.

</Phase>
