Bring to mind a course — any course, not necessarily about meditation — that left you feeling handled by a procedure rather than spoken to.

Don't analyse what was wrong with it. **Picture instead a house you were let into where nobody showed you the exit.** The lights are on, the furniture is arranged, everything is correct — but no one said what to do if the air gets close. And there's no one to ask: the host leaves in a week, and next week it will be a different host.

Hold that feeling for a moment. You'll need it exactly once — right now — so that the difference in how this course is built becomes visible.

<Phase type="reflection">

Now change the lens. Not a house — **a medicine package.**

There are always two texts on it: what the drug does, and what it will not do, at which signs to stop, and whom to go to.

It's strange that courses about the mind almost never print the second half. Meditation is sold on the first half only: calm, clarity, productivity. About "stop and seek help" — silence.

**Notice that you're holding a course that begins with the second half.** Not because we love disclaimers, but because the frame in which a person meets a difficult experience changes how that experience goes on from there.

</Phase>

<Phase type="concept">

## What this course is

Eight steps of attention practice. Each one is a short piece of theory, one sitting session, and a conversation about what happened in it: with an AI interlocutor between sessions, and with living people at the end of the road.

The course **draws on** approaches studied in mindfulness-based stress reduction programmes, but **is not** any of them: MBSR and MBCT are authorised programmes with a protected curriculum, and calling your own course by their name without authorisation is not allowed.

## What the research shows — unrounded

The largest review assembled for medical decision-making (Goyal et al., *JAMA Internal Medicine*, 2014; 47 clinical trials, 3,515 people) gives mindfulness programmes a **small effect**: anxiety d≈0.38, depression d≈0.30, pain d≈0.33 — against active controls. Superiority over physical exercise or psychotherapy was **not shown**.

That is the honest anchor. If somebody promises you "40% less anxiety in eight weeks", such percentages almost always come from a single study with a convenient scale.

## What the course does not do

- **It does not treat and does not replace therapy.** If things are hard right now, this course is not instead of a professional — at best it stands beside one.
- **It does not work with trauma.** Practice can surface traumatic material; working with that requires a clinical context.
- **It does not promise states.** No proven trajectory of "week N → state M" exists.
- **It does not assume more is better.** Rising intensity and retreats appear on the list of risk factors.

## Risks named before the practice, not after

When the adverse effects of meditation began to be measured seriously, the picture came out like this: in the study by Britton and colleagues (*Clinical Psychological Science*, 2021; 96 participants, independent interviewer), **83%** reported at least one unpleasant effect, and for **37%** it affected everyday functioning. In a US population sample (Goldberg et al., 2022; 434 people) — about a third, and re-experiencing traumatic material is among the frequent ones.

For a long time these numbers didn't exist for a simple reason: **84% of clinical trials never asked about adverse events at all** (Wong et al., cited in Baer et al., 2019). "No side effects found" meant "nobody looked".

That is not a reason to avoid practising. It is a reason to know in advance what "time to stop" looks like.

## Who may need company for this format

Not a ban and not a filter — routing. If at least one of these is true of you, the honest path is to start with a living professional beside you rather than alone:

- a psychiatric diagnosis, current or past;
- a history of trauma or severe childhood adversity;
- current states where the sense of reality or of yourself slips;
- thoughts of harming yourself;
- a recent acute bereavement.

## Stop signals

End the session and return to ordinary things if any of these appear: mounting panic, a sense that you or the world are unreal, an inrush of images from the past that you cannot halt, trembling and an inability to bring attention back. This is not a failure of practice and does not mean you are "not trying properly". It means the dose is too large right now.

**If the heaviness persists beyond the session, contact a living professional.** Neither this text nor the AI interlocutor later in the course is crisis care.

</Phase>

<Phase type="practice">

**First session — ten minutes.** No more, even if it feels like you could go longer: the only study found that compared 10 and 30 minutes a day found no difference by dose, while risk rises with intensity.

The order is this:

1. Sit so your back holds itself and your body isn't fighting. A chair works.
2. Set a timer for 10 minutes. Eyes can stay half-open — gaze resting on the floor a metre ahead.
3. Find the breath where it registers most clearly: nostrils, chest, or belly. Don't change it.
4. When you notice you've gone off into thought, simply come back to the breath. Noticing and returning *is* the exercise, not a mistake inside it.
5. When the timer sounds, sit for a few seconds more before standing.

After the session, open the course journal (or a plain file) and answer three questions for yourself: what was happening in the body, where did attention most often go, was anything unpleasant.

**Talking about the session.** Press "Learn with AI" below — the companion will go through those three answers with you. It doesn't grade your practice and doesn't diagnose; its job is to ask the questions you didn't ask yourself. What it can and cannot do — we'll cover in detail in step seven.

</Phase>
