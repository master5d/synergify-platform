<Phase type="activation">

Recall the last time you said something important out loud and heard silence in return — not empty silence, but attentive. No advice, no "I understand you", no answering story. Just a person who held what was said and didn't hurry to fix it.

If nothing like that comes to mind, recall the opposite: saying something real and getting advice back.

**Don't judge now who was in the wrong.** Register only the physical difference between "I was heard" and "I was processed".

This is the last step of the course, and it is entirely about that difference.

</Phase>

<Phase type="reflection">

Change the lens. Not a "support group", but **a belay on a rock face.**

The belayer doesn't climb for you. They don't decide where to put your foot and don't cheer every metre. Almost all of the time they simply hold the rope and watch. From outside it looks like they're doing nothing.

The value of a belay is visible exactly once — in the second of a fall. The rest of the time it looks superfluous, which is precisely why it is so easy to treat as an optional extra.

**Notice that solitary practice is climbing without a belay, where most of the time nothing does in fact happen.** Courses that sell practice as an app are selling that "most of the time".

</Phase>

<Phase type="concept">

## Why a circle rather than another app

There is no direct study of "group versus no group" in this field — and pretending otherwise would be a lie. There are three indirect lines, and they converge:

1. **Guided versus unguided.** Meta-analyses of digital interventions: guided formats **g≈0.83** [0.43–1.22] against **g≈0.27** [0.08–0.46] for unguided; even a **non-clinician** guide improves outcomes (*JMIR*, 2022: g = −0.17 [−0.23…−0.11] in favour of guidance).
2. **Online works — with a person.** **Segal and colleagues (*JAMA Psychiatry*, 2020; 460 participants)**: an online programme outperformed usual care, **and it ran with coach support**, not as a purely self-guided app.
3. **What works inside a group isn't "group energy".** **Bisseling and colleagues (*Clinical Psychology & Psychotherapy*, 2019)**: the predictor of improvement turned out to be the **therapeutic alliance** — the relationship; group cohesion and facilitator competence were not predictors. A mandatory caveat: this is a single study in a single population (cancer patients) and cannot be generalised.

What follows honestly: the evidence supports not "the group as such" but **a living person in the loop**. A circle is the most available form of such a person when there is neither a coach nor a therapist.

And the same thing from the other side: self-directed formats produce **more attrition** (reviews of unguided self-help), and in the controlled dose study attrition ran to **40.5%** (Fincham, 2023). Being alone isn't only about effect — it's about whether a person gets to the end at all.

## How to assemble a circle that stands a chance

Four rules. They're dull, and that is their merit.

- **A small group: three to seven people.** More than that and the word stops reaching everyone; it ceases to be a circle and becomes a broadcast.
- **Everyone speaks, in turn, uninterrupted.** Not "who'd like to share", but by rotation — including those who will say two sentences today.
- **Rotating roles.** Whoever runs the meeting changes. A permanent facilitator is already a vertical, and a vertical in such a group soon starts deciding whose experience is correct.
- **The right to leave in silence.** To skip a circle, to go halfway through, to pass on your turn — with no explanation and no consequence. A format where refusal must be justified reproduces exactly the frame that, in *Transcultural Psychiatry* (2022), lengthens difficulties.

And one thing a circle does not do: it doesn't interpret anyone's experience and doesn't treat its members. A circle is not a therapy group. If somebody has more surfacing than fits into an evening, the circle's job is not to "work it through" but to help them get to a professional.

If you have no frame of your own for a meeting, there's a ready one: **the Group Insight Game (GII)** — six cards, four steps, one turn each around the circle. It was built for the academy's study groups, but it works for a practice circle too: the cards keep the conversation from collapsing into advice.

## How to choose a school or a course

Seven signs that a format is built dishonestly. Not every one of them alone means fraud, but each is a question worth asking out loud before handing over your time.

1. **Nothing is said about risks and adverse effects.** A course that talks only about benefit reproduces the field's own defect: **84% of 231 randomised trials didn't report adverse events at all** (Wong et al., 2018, via the review by Baer et al., *Clinical Psychology Review*, 2019).
2. **No pre-screening and no informed consent.** Baer and colleagues (2019) recommend outright that both benefits and risks be discussed before the start.
3. **Monitoring is purely passive** — "write to us if it gets hard". The position of Cheetah House, the organisation that helps people with meditation-related difficulties: passive monitoring is not adequate.
4. **The teacher has no traceable training.** A validated instrument for assessing teaching exists — **the MBI:TAC (Crane et al., *Psychological Assessment*, 2013)**, six domains of competence. The question "what standard were you trained against?" is always fair.
5. **An effect size is promised that isn't in the literature.** The real anchor is d≈0.2–0.4 and no demonstrated superiority over physical exercise or psychotherapy (Goyal et al., *JAMA Internal Medicine*, 2014).
6. **The course positions itself as treatment or as a stand-in for therapy**, or the teacher presents as a therapist without a licence.
7. **Beginners are driven straight into an intensive or a retreat.** Intensity and retreats are on the list of risk factors (Matko and Van Dam, 2026).

An eighth, informal one: **there's no humane way out of the course.** If declining to practise is read as your malfunction, that's the same frame as rule four of the circle.

## How this course ends

With nothing. There is no second tier here, no advanced module, no "level for those who are ready". Everything the course had, it has given: an anchor in the breath, a shortened body pass with eyes open, light naming, a frame for the difficult, the distinction between awareness and sealing over, honest limits on AI, and the construction of a circle.

After that: three to seven living people and one evening a week.

**The course succeeded at the moment it became unnecessary.** A practice that binds you to its source is built the same way as the format from step six: pleasant, smooth, and going nowhere. If in six months you're sitting with people you found yourself, following rules the two of you rewrote to fit, and you can't remember where it all came from — then it worked.

</Phase>

<Phase type="practice">

**The last session — 15 minutes, built like a circle meeting, only alone for now.**

1. Sit. Eyes open, soft gaze. Timer — 15 minutes.
2. The first seven minutes: whichever practice from the course is yours — breath (step 2), a shortened body pass (step 3), or breath with naming (step 4). Choose the one that turned out to be yours over these weeks.
3. The rules of step five apply: the edge of the window, stop signals, the right to finish early.
4. The next three minutes: silence with no assignment. Just sit.
5. The last five minutes: **a circle of one.** Say aloud, into the empty room, three things: what happened in today's practice, what over these eight steps turned out to be the most uncomfortable, and one thing you'd want to say to people if they turn up. Aloud, not inwardly — that matters: the difference between a thought and a spoken word is what a circle assembles for.
6. Stand and put those same three things in the journal, along with the names of two or three people you could in principle invite to sit together.

**Talking about the session.** Press "Learn with AI" one last time — and use the companion strictly for what step seven allows: ask it to help you assemble a short invitation out of your three things, two or three sentences you wouldn't be embarrassed to send a living person. Not "compose it for me" and not "assess whether I'm ready"; just help bring what's already said into shape.

And sending it — that part you do without it.

</Phase>
