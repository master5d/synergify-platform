<Phase type="activation">

Recall a thought that circled this week longer than you needed it to. Not the heaviest one — the stickiest. A conversation you replayed for both sides. A sentence you should have said. A to-do list run through a third time without a single thing done.

Don't decide now whether it was right. **Register one thing only: it arrived on its own.** You didn't sit down to think it. It was an event that happened, not a decision you made.

Hold that distinction for a few seconds. This whole step is about it.

</Phase>

<Phase type="reflection">

Change the lens. Not "thoughts in your head", but **announcements at a railway station.**

The speaker says: train to Rostov, platform three. That is not an order to go to Rostov. It's a message that such a train exists. You can board it, you can stay on the bench, you can simply register that the announcement was made.

**Notice what happens when a thought stops being a command and becomes an announcement.** There's no point arguing with the loudspeaker. No point proving to it that Rostov isn't needed. Between "it sounded" and "I went" a gap appears, one breath wide — and that gap is the only thing being trained here.

</Phase>

<Phase type="concept">

## What naming is

Naming (in the English-language literature, *noting*) is a short label for what is happening in the mind right now, in a single word: "planning", "remembering", "sound", "itch", "anxiety", "rehearsing a conversation".

There is one rule and it is strict: **the word must be short, neutral, and said inwardly once.** Not "here I am again chewing on that conversation for twenty minutes, how stupid of me" — that isn't naming, that's the second episode of the same series. Just "rehearsing" — and back to the breath.

Naming belongs to the set of practices that a beginner online may be given — **with caveats**. The caveat is exactly the one attached to the body scan in the previous step: the practice sharpens observation of internal states, and for someone with a trauma history that carries the same risk profile. So: short, light, with the right to stop.

## The line that must not be crossed here

There is a version of this technique that is not given at all in a beginner course without a teacher present: **continuous intensive noting in the style of long retreats, aimed at "stages of insight" and passing through the "dark night".**

The grounds for that exclusion are named directly:

- **Lindahl, Fisher, Cooper, Rosen and Britton (*PLOS ONE*, 2017)** — a qualitative study of 60 practitioners and 32 experts, 59 categories of experience across 7 domains. One of those domains is the **sense of self**, and it contains depersonalisation and derealisation outright. Practices that deliberately work on dissolving the sense of self are not material for a self-directed online course.
- **Britton and colleagues (*Clinical Psychological Science*, 2021)** — the marker of persistent bad effects turned out to be signs of **arousal dysregulation**; aggressive continuous noting is precisely a practice that revs things up.
- **Matko and Van Dam (*Current Opinion in Psychology*, 2026)** — intensity and retreats on the list of risk factors.

An important caveat about Lindahl 2017: those are **not population frequencies**. The sample was recruited on the basis of having had difficulties, so its numbers give a map of phenomena, not probabilities. Quoting them as "the risk of meditation" isn't allowed, and we don't.

The practical conclusion is simple: **naming here is a light label for ten minutes, not an instrument of assault.** If an eagerness appears to "label everything, miss nothing", that's a signal to ease off, not a sign of progress.

## Why this isn't fighting with thoughts

Because fighting doesn't work and isn't the goal. A thought does not need to be chased away, refuted, swapped for a correct one, or "released" by effort. Naming does exactly one thing: it moves a thought out of the status "what I currently am" into the status "what is currently happening".

Hence the key distinction we'll return to in step five: **the label "anxiety" is not a diagnosis and not a verdict.** It's a statement about the weather.

</Phase>

<Phase type="practice">

**Session — 12 minutes.** Breath stays the base; naming is a layer on top of it.

1. Sit. Eyes open or half-open, gaze on the floor. Timer — 12 minutes.
2. The first three minutes: breath only, as in step two. Don't rush into labelling.
3. Then: whenever you notice you've gone off, **one word inwardly**, quiet and short. "Planning." "Remembering." "Sound." "Itching." "Bored." "Anxious."
4. The word is said once. If a second one wants to follow, that's already a thought about a thought; label it "commenting" and return to the breath.
5. If no word comes, say "this" and return. Precision isn't needed here.
6. If you catch eagerness ("now I'll label everything"), ease off. Go back to plain breath for a minute.
7. The last two minutes: breath only again, no labels.
8. At the chime, sit a few seconds before standing.

After the session, record in the journal: which three or four words recurred most often, whether there was a word after which things got uncomfortable, and what the body was doing in the moments of naming.

**Talking about the session.** Press "Learn with AI" and hand it your list of recurring words. A useful question: "what does my set of labels say about what my days are full of?" — recurring labels usually describe not the practice but the life around it. The companion here helps you lay observations out rather than decode them: it does not interpret what frequent anxiety "means", and it does not set you tasks to increase the dose of practice.

</Phase>
