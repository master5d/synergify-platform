<Phase type="activation">

Recall a moment when you were breathing and knew it. Not in practice — in ordinary life. Before walking into an office. After nearly missing a step on the stairs. In the first seconds after an argument, when the words have run out.

Don't analyse what that was. **Just observe this: breath is the only thing in the body that runs by itself yet can be noticed in any given second.** You can't reach the heart that way. You can't notice digestion that way.

Hold that for a few breaths. The whole course leans on that availability.

</Phase>

<Phase type="reflection">

Now change the lens. Not an "exercise", but **an anchor in a roadstead.**

An anchor takes the boat nowhere. It doesn't improve the weather or flatten the swell. It does one thing: it keeps you from drifting off. The boat still swings in a circle around it, pulls the chain taut, rocks — that is the normal working of a vessel at anchor, not a malfunction.

**Notice how the expectation shifts if the breath is an anchor rather than an engine.** Then "my mind wandered off again" stops being a failure: the chain went taut, the boat came back. That is precisely what the exercise is for.

</Phase>

<Phase type="concept">

## Why the breath specifically

Of all anchors for attention, breath is the most studied and the least risky. In safety reviews of what is appropriate for a beginner online, mindful breathing in short sessions sits in the baseline section rather than the qualified one: it doesn't require prolonged immersion in bodily sensation and doesn't work on the sense of self.

The technique has an origin: in the Pali tradition it is called *ānāpānasati* — literally, attention on the in-breath and out-breath. We take only the mechanics and describe them in secular terms. The ancient texts themselves are in the public domain; their translations are not, which is why there isn't a single quotation from someone else's translation here.

## "More" does not mean "better"

This is the place where nearly every course lies by default.

The only study found that compared doses directly is **Fincham, Mavor and Dritschel (*Mindfulness*, 2023; an online dose-ranging randomised trial)**. They compared roughly 10 minutes against roughly 29 minutes of sitting practice (and 9 against 30 minutes of movement practice) over 14 days. **All four conditions improved, and there was no significant difference by dose.**

Two details from the same study that usually don't get retold:

- adherence was about 7–8 days out of 14, roughly half, and overall attrition was **40.5%**;
- **short sessions were not practised more often than long ones.** The common assumption that "make it shorter and people will do it more" was directly not supported here.

The honest conclusion is single: **the field gives no dose–response curve with a threshold of "so many minutes".** The canonical 45 minutes in older programmes is an inheritance of protocol, not an established optimum. And "10 minutes is enough" has not been proven by anyone either.

So why do we still set a ceiling? Because the list of risk factors for adverse effects includes **rising intensity and retreats** (review by Matko and Van Dam, *Current Opinion in Psychology*, 2026). Causation there is not established — it is a stable association, and the authors say so themselves. But when there is no evidence that "more is better" and there is a signal that "more is riskier", the conservative choice is obvious.

Hence, in this course: **10–20 minutes, with a ceiling for all eight weeks of no more than 30–45 minutes per session. No home retreats, no days of silence, no "hundred days in a row".**

## What to do with a wandering mind

Nothing special. The mind goes off — that's its job, not a symptom.

The practice has four beats, and the third and fourth matter more than the first two: breath → gone → **noticed** → **returned**. Noticing and returning is the repetition of the exercise. Somebody whose attention wandered forty times did forty repetitions; somebody for whom "it was all smooth" has usually just dozed off.

One caveat you'll need later: **the breath does not need fixing.** Not deeper, not more even, not "into the belly". Practices with breath-holding or forced breathing belong to a different physiology and a different risk profile; they are not here.

## A missed day

A skipped day is not a failure. With 40% attrition in a controlled study (Fincham, 2023), a missed day is statistically normal human behaviour rather than a character defect. Coming back tomorrow for ten minutes beats "making up" forty.

</Phase>

<Phase type="practice">

**Session — 10 minutes.** If after the first week you want more, you can go to 15–20. No further.

1. Sit so your back holds itself and your body isn't fighting. A chair works; so does its back.
2. Set a timer for 10 minutes. Eyes open or half-open — gaze unfocused on the floor a metre ahead.
3. For the first three breaths, simply register that you have sat down. No technique, no task.
4. Find the place where the breath registers most clearly: nostrils, chest, or belly. Pick one and stay there for the whole session.
5. Change nothing about the breathing. The task is to notice it, not to improve it.
6. When you find yourself in thought, mark it inwardly as "gone" and return to your chosen place. As many times as it takes.
7. If you feel the pull to open your eyes wider, shift, or look around the room — do it. You won't break the practice; you'll bring yourself back into it.
8. At the chime, sit a few seconds more before standing.

After the session, open the course journal and record three things: where the breath registered most clearly, roughly how many times attention wandered (a rough estimate, no counting), and whether anything was unpleasant.

</Phase>
