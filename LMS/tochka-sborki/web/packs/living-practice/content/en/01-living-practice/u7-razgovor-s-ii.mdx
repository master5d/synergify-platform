<Phase type="activation">

Recall a conversation after which you felt lighter — and which changed nothing. Somebody agreed with everything you said. Confirmed you were right. Sympathised.

Now recall a conversation after which you felt awkward — and after which you did something differently. There was a question in it you'd have preferred to dodge.

**Don't decide which of the two was better.** Register only that these are two different products, and that the first feels nicer at exactly the moment the second is more useful.

Hold that distinction. This whole step concerns a tool that is very good at the first one.

</Phase>

<Phase type="reflection">

Change the lens. Not an interlocutor, but **a rear-view mirror.**

A mirror shows you what you can't see yourself, and that is its entire value. It doesn't drive the car, doesn't choose the route, doesn't decide whether the trip is worth making. It has no view of what lies ahead and knows nothing about where you're headed.

There's a second feature too: **a mirror won't notice that you're in a bad way.** It reflects, and that's all.

**Notice how your relationship to an object changes once its function is named precisely.** Nobody resents a mirror for not steering. The trouble starts when it gets treated as a second driver.

</Phase>

<Phase type="concept">

## Start with the most inconvenient fact

The evidence says that what works in digital programmes is **a human in the loop**.

Meta-analyses of digital interventions give a stable difference: guided formats **g≈0.83** [0.43–1.22] against unguided **g≈0.27** [0.08–0.46]. And even a **non-clinician** guide improves outcomes: a *JMIR* review (2022) gives **Hedges g = −0.17** [−0.23…−0.11] in favour of guidance.

The largest online randomised trial in this area — **Segal and colleagues (*JAMA Psychiatry*, 2020; 460 participants)** — showed an advantage of an online programme over usual care. The detail usually lost in retellings: **the programme ran with coach support, not as a purely self-guided app.**

Self-guided formats work too — just more weakly, and with more attrition (a review of unguided self-help programmes, *Clinical Psychology Review*, 2021: small but significant effects).

Hence the course's honest position: **AI sits here between sessions, not instead of people.** It covers the stretch where you're alone with your journal; it does not cover the role of the living human this course is walking you towards in step eight.

## What the companion may do

The boundaries aren't invented by the course — they're assembled from normative documents and from the few places where an effect has actually been shown:

- **Unpacking a session.** You bring observations; it hands them back sorted and asks the questions you didn't ask yourself.
- **A journal and structured reflection against a template** — "what was in the body", "what was hard", "where you were relative to the window".
- **Reviewing course material.** What an anchor of attention is, how naming differs from fighting, why there's a ceiling on duration here. Psychoeducation on prepared material is the niche where chatbot effects have actually been shown (*JMIR*, 2022 and 2025).
- **Reminders, planning, tracking.** An administrative function — explicitly permitted even in the strictest jurisdiction.
- **Navigation.** "Here's the part of the course about that", "here's where to turn". In *Nature Medicine* (2026), a randomised trial of a chatbot easing transitions between levels of care showed benefit precisely in **routing**, not in therapy.
- **Preparing what you'll say in the circle.** Turning a jumble into two or three sentences you wouldn't be ashamed to say out loud to living people. This is arguably the companion's main function in this course.

## What the companion may not do — and why

The **American Psychological Association Health Advisory on generative AI chatbots and wellness applications (November 2025)** is the weightiest normative source available today. The mechanisms of harm it names:

- **Sycophancy.** Systems "engineered for maximum engagement" and trained to **validate user input** rather than offer therapeutic challenge — which, in the APA's phrasing, can "lock them into a cycle that exacerbates their mental illness". This is exactly why the conversation from step six — about sealing over and your own place in what's happening — goes worse with a model than with people: it will agree.
- **A false alliance.** A direct warning against "creating a false sense of therapeutic alliance"; a relationship with AI is "one-sided, even if the user perceives otherwise".
- **Dependency.** The risk of "foster[ing] unhealthy dependencies by blurring the lines between a relationship with a digital tool and a human relationship".
- **Recommendation 1**, stated categorically: chatbots and wellness apps "should not be used as a replacement for a qualified mental health care provider", though they "may be appropriate as a supportive **adjunct, not substitute**, to an ongoing therapeutic relationship".

The second fact concerns conversation length. Following a lawsuit brought by a teenager's family, OpenAI publicly acknowledged that safeguards "work more reliably in common, short exchanges", while in long interactions "parts of the model's safety training may degrade". That is a vendor statement rather than research — an admission against interest — and it bears directly on our construction: **an eight-week course with a companion is precisely a long interaction.** Hence the rule: short conversations about one session, not an endless thread about your life.

The third fact is legal. The **Illinois WOPR Act (Wellness and Oversight for Psychological Resources, PA 104-0054, signed 1 August 2025)** made Illinois the first US state to prohibit AI from providing psychotherapy, diagnosis, treatment planning, **crisis intervention** and therapeutic support, and from advertising chatbots as a stand-in for a licensed professional; penalties reach $10,000. Administrative and supportive tasks under a licensed professional's oversight remain permitted. Similar restrictions passed in **Nevada and Utah** in 2025. So the boundary "AI does not conduct a crisis conversation" is not our caution but, in some jurisdictions, active law.

## The blocklist, briefly

The companion in this course does **not**:

- conduct a crisis conversation or assess risk of self-harm;
- diagnose or interpret symptoms;
- present itself as a therapist, a specialist curator, or something that "understands you the way a person would";
- work with traumatic material;
- push you to increase the duration or intensity of practice;
- act as the only channel of feedback in the course.

And one more thing worth saying plainly: **it does not read your state.** It sees only the words you gave it. If things are heavy between your lines, between the lines is exactly where it won't look.

## Whom to call instead

With thoughts of self-harm, a persistent sense of unreality, or unusual perceptions — leave the course and reach a living person. In the US, the **988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline**; in another country, the local crisis line. This is the one part of the course that doesn't wait for the next step.

</Phase>

<Phase type="practice">

**Two parts today: a 10-minute session and a conversation of about ten minutes.**

Practice first:

1. Sit, eyes open, timer — 10 minutes. Breath as in step two, naming as in step four — one short label whenever you drift.
2. The rules of step five apply: the edge of the window, stop signals, the right to finish early.
3. At the chime, sit a few seconds and stand.

Then record four lines in the journal: where the breath was, the three most frequent labels, what was in the body, whether anything was unpleasant.

**Talking about the session.** Press "Learn with AI" and open the conversation with exactly this frame — it assigns the companion a role rather than merely asking a question:

> I'm working through a course on attention practice. Today's session was 10 minutes: breath plus short naming. Here are my notes: [the four lines from the journal].
>
> Help me go through this session. Ask one question at a time. Don't interpret what my sensations "mean", don't set me tasks, and don't suggest practising longer. If there's anything in my words you'd agree with out of habit, say so directly. At the end, help me assemble two or three sentences from this that I could say out loud to living people.

That last sentence isn't decoration. It is the companion's work in this course: bringing your experience to a form in which it can be carried out to people. Where to carry it — step eight.

</Phase>
