<Phase type="activation">

You fine-tuned a model and asked it about three examples from your dataset. All three answers are perfect, word for word as in the labels.

The first reaction is joy. The second, if you listen closely, is suspicion.

What exactly did this check show: that the model learned the task — or that it memorized the answers?

</Phase>

<Phase type="reflection">

You know the difference between "understood" and "memorized" from school. A student who learned the answers to the exam cards will ace those cards and fail on a new question.

It's the same with a model. Checking on training examples measures memory, not skill.

Consider: which examples from your task could the model definitely not have learned from?

</Phase>

<Phase type="concept">

## Holdout: set it aside before you start

The main rule of evaluation: **some examples are set aside before training and never shown to the model**. This is the **holdout** set.

Data is usually split into three parts:

- **train** — the model learns from these;
- **validation** — you tune settings on these along the way;
- **test** — you look at it once, at the end.

If you edited the data while looking at errors on test, it has become validation. An honest evaluation needs a new one.

## Overfitting

**Overfitting** — the model fits the training examples so tightly that it does worse on new ones. The signal in the logs: loss on train keeps falling, while on validation it stops falling or rises.

What helps: more varied examples, fewer epochs (passes over the data), early stopping on validation.

Fine-tuning also has a flip side — **catastrophic forgetting**. The model learned your format and unlearned what it could do before. So check old skills too, not only the new one.

## Benchmark versus your task

Public **benchmarks** measure general skills: knowledge, reasoning, code. They are useful for comparing models with each other.

But they have two limits for you:

1. A benchmark doesn't know your task. Gains on it don't promise gains on your tickets.
2. The texts of popular benchmarks may have ended up in models' training data. Then a high score partly measures memory.

So for your task, build **your own evaluation set** — from the same holdout.

## How to tell whether fine-tuning helped

1. **Criteria before the run.** Decide in advance what counts as a good answer: format kept, field extracted correctly, tone fits.
2. **The same holdout set** goes through the model "before" and "after."
3. **Compare by criteria**, not by impression. If you can, look at the answers without knowing which model produced them.
4. **Check that nothing broke:** a couple of questions on general skills.
5. **Compare with the cheap alternative:** the same original model with a good prompt from module 04. If the difference is small, fine-tuning didn't pay off.

The size of the evaluation set depends on the task. For a first estimate, a few dozen examples will do. That's an order-of-magnitude estimate, not a norm.
