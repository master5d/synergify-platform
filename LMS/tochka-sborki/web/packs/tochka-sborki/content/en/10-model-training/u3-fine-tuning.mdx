## When NOT to fine-tune

Climb the ladder from the bottom and stop at the first rung that solves the task:

1. **Prompt** — a clear spec, examples in the request, an answer format (module 04).
2. **Context and RAG** — search your documents for the needed pieces and place them in the prompt.
3. **Tools and agents** — give the model a calculator, search, a checker; split the task into roles (modules 07–08).
4. **Fine-tuning** — only if rungs 1–3 hit a ceiling and you have data.

Why this order. The first three rungs change in minutes and roll back with a single edit. Fine-tuning needs a dataset, a run, and a fresh evaluation after every change.

And a separate note on facts. Fresh and changing knowledge is more practical to keep in context: update the document and the answer updates. Weights don't update that way.

## When fine-tuning is justified

- You need a **stable format or style** that the prompt holds unreliably.
- You need a **narrow skill** on your own data: ticket classification, field extraction.
- You need a **small model** that runs locally, fast and cheap — and it has to be brought up to the task.

## SFT: learning from examples

**SFT** (supervised fine-tuning) is training on "request → desired answer" pairs. The mechanics are the same as pretraining: predict the next token. Only now the text is yours, and the error is counted on the answer.

This is how a base model is turned into an assistant. The same way, you can teach a small model your format.

## LoRA and PEFT: training only a part

Full fine-tuning changes all of the model's weights and needs a lot of memory. **PEFT** (parameter-efficient fine-tuning) is a family of methods that train only a small part.

The most common is **LoRA** ([Hu et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.09685)). The original weights are frozen, and small **adapter** matrices are trained alongside them. An adapter is orders of magnitude smaller than the model, easy to store, swap and roll back.

The **QLoRA** variant also compresses (quantizes) the frozen base so it fits on a modest GPU.

## Choosing a small model

For learning and narrow tasks, take a model of the **SmolLM2** class from Hugging Face: 135M, 360M or 1.7B parameters, Apache-2.0 license. M means millions of parameters, B means billions.

- **Base** or **Instruct**: for a dialogue format start with Instruct — post-training has already been done for you.
- A small model knows little about the world. It becomes strong only on a narrow task with good examples.
- The smaller the model, the faster the run and the more experiments you fit in. For a first time, that's enough.
